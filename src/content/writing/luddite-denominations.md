It continues to prove impossible to stop thinking about AI, because it does unfortunately seem to be an epochal shift in human technology or whatever. I am (again, with great pain and sadness) becoming persuaded that the way software is made at serious scale has been changed forever. It has changed in the same way that manufacturing was changed by precision CNC and 3D printing, the way transport was changed by personal motor cars, the way textile manufacturing was changed by steam-powered mills. There is now simply a faster way to do it, and any society which economises in the face of scarcity (i.e. any society we can imagine) will use that way now that it is known.

In talking to friends through Permacomputing, I know that people disagree with this view. And I've been interested to find that part of the nub of the issue is a difference in view of technological progress, and of what was at stake in the Luddite resistance. My knowledge of this movement is of course based on [[blood-in-the-machine.md]], which (in my reading) has the thesis that the Luddites did not resist the technological change _per se_; they oppose the social impact that its emergence threatened to have in the hands of the powers of the day. That is, they did not contend that we should un-learn the technology, but that if it was to be part of our society, then it had to be on equitable terms.

I think that I am following this ethos in saying that I am opposed to AI _the industry_, but that I think it is highly non-obvious to conclude that there is no possible integration of AI _the technology_ with society that would be just or ethical (see [[ai-valid-possible-futures.md#Disambiguating]]). But I have encountered people whose opinions I respect a lot who seem to take the opposite view: that my argument is naively accepting the inevitabilist framing of the tech industry, and that it is legitimate to contend that not all technological discoveries should be put into effect in society. This is something I want to think about a lot more.
